Origin and History of LibreOffice

The LibreOffice project emerged as an independent, community-driven fork of OpenOffice.org following corporate shifts that threatened the suite's open-source future. Tracing its lineage back to the 1980s software StarOffice, LibreOffice was formally established in 2010 by The Document Foundation to ensure a free, vendor-neutral office suite. Today, it stands as one of the most widely used open-source office productivity suites in the world.

The Roots: StarOffice and Sun Microsystems

The underlying foundation of LibreOffice began in 1985 with the development of StarOffice by the German company Star Division. In 1999, Sun Microsystems acquired Star Division to provide an alternative to Microsoft Office. In 2000, Sun released the source code of StarOffice to the public under open-source licenses, establishing the OpenOffice.org project. For the next decade, OpenOffice.org served as the premier open-source office suite, maintained primarily by Sun employees alongside an active community of external contributors.

The Catalyst: The Oracle Acquisition

In January 2010, Oracle Corporation acquired Sun Microsystems. The acquisition created immediate tension within the OpenOffice.org community. Contributors grew concerned about Oracle's commitment to open-source governance, slow code integration, and the centralized management of the project. Attempts to negotiate a more independent, community-led governance model with Oracle were unsuccessful.

The 2010 Fork and The Document Foundation

On September 28, 2010, key members of the OpenOffice.org community announced the creation of The Document Foundation (TDF) and initiated a fork of the codebase named LibreOffice. The name combined the French/Spanish word libre (free) with the English word office.

The Document Foundation was established as an independent entity based in Germany to manage development, promote the OpenDocument Format (ODF), and prevent any single corporate sponsor from dominating the project. TDF initially invited Oracle to join the foundation and donate the OpenOffice.org trademark; Oracle declined and subsequently requested that members involved with TDF resign from the OpenOffice.org Council.

Adoption and Evolution

LibreOffice quickly gained momentum. Major Linux distributions—including Ubuntu, Fedora, openSUSE, and Debian—replaced OpenOffice.org with LibreOffice as their default productivity suite. Developers focused on aggressively refactoring the codebase, eliminating thousands of lines of obsolete code, improving performance, and enhancing compatibility with Microsoft Office formats.

LibreOffice 3.3, the project's first official release, debuted in January 2011. In June 2011, Oracle ceased commercial development of OpenOffice and transferred its code and trademarks to the Apache Software Foundation, where it became Apache OpenOffice. However, the majority of active developers, Linux distributions, and corporate sponsors had already migrated to LibreOffice.

Through continuous semi-annual releases, LibreOffice has grown to support dozens of languages and modern operating systems, remaining the flagship standard for free and open-source office productivity software.
